Ecuador’s Mangroves: A Big Chance to Protect and Restore the Coast

A new report says Ecuador has strong laws and room to bring mangrove forests back. It says the next step is making the protections work on the ground.

WhereQuito, Ecuador (mangroves along Ecuador’s Pacific coast)
When26 October 2026
AuthorNot listed (published by Wetlands International)
SourceWetlands International, News

QUITO, Ecuador — Ecuador has a major chance to protect and bring back its mangrove forests. That is the message of a new report called “Mobilizing the Mangrove Breakthrough in Ecuador.” The report says the country already has strong laws. It says the next job is to make those laws work well in real places.

Past & Present: Comparing the Ecosystem

A mangrove is a coastal forest of trees that grow along the shore. Mangroves in Ecuador are in six major estuarine systems. An estuary is a place where a river meets the sea. Biodiversity means the many kinds of living things in one place. The article says Ecuador has some of the tallest and most biodiverse mangrove forests in the Americas.

  • They cover about 1,550 km². That is the size of Rome.
  • They cover 36% of Ecuador’s Pacific coastline.
  • They support centuries-old fisheries for crab, shellfish and finfish.

Mangroves are smaller than they used to be. Since 1969, Ecuador has lost nearly 23% of its mangroves. The article gives this number from a source called CLIRSEN.

Who lives in Ecuador’s mangroves? Mangrove trees include the red, black and white mangrove. Red mangrove crabs live in muddy burrows among the roots, and people catch them for food. Shrimp, oysters, conchs, mussels and fish such as mullet grow up in mangroves. More than 40 kinds of birds, including brown pelicans and frigatebirds, live in the forests. An older report counted about 204,000 hectares of mangroves in Ecuador in 1969. That is about 2,040 km², compared with about 1,550 km² today.

Human Impact: What Changed and Why?

The article says people’s actions caused mangrove loss. A human pressure is something people do that puts stress on nature. Here are the pressures the article names:

  • Shrimp farming. In the past, shrimp farms grew larger and caused a lot of mangrove loss. About 8,000 hectares are still under pressure from this, mostly in northern Esmeraldas and the Gulf of Guayaquil.
  • Agriculture. Farming also changes the land.
  • Cities growing. This is happening around Guayaquil, Machala and Esmeraldas.
  • Pollution and overfishing.

The article also reports one hopeful change. New technology, rules and management have made the shrimp industry more productive. The article says this creates chances to balance shrimp farming with protecting and restoring mangroves.

Climate Change & Consequences

The article does not say that climate change is harming Ecuador’s mangroves. It does explain what mangroves do for people:

  • They protect 3.4 million people who live on the coast.
  • They prevent about $250 million in flood losses each year.
  • They help keep coastal food supplies safe. Fishing families depend on them.

Losing mangroves would mean losing this protection. The article does not say that directly, but it describes these benefits as coming from mangroves. It also mentions national climate and biodiversity plans, and “blue-carbon” and climate-finance ideas for raising money.

What is “blue carbon”? Mangroves, salt marshes and seagrass meadows take carbon dioxide out of the air and store it in their plants and soil for a long time. Carbon stored in coastal places like these is called blue carbon. If these places are damaged, the carbon can go back into the air.

How might climate change affect mangroves? Rising seas, stronger storms and changes in how salty the water is can make it harder for mangroves to survive. This is a risk for the people who depend on mangroves for food and protection.

Solutions & Shared Responsibility

The article lists many solutions. Here are the main ones:

  • Strong laws. Mangroves are protected by Ecuador’s Constitution, its Organic Environmental Code and several decrees. They are also protected by the Ramsar Convention.
  • Protected areas. A protected area is land with rules that keep nature safe. About 46% of mangrove cover is in Protected Areas.
  • Community custody. Agreements called AUSCEMs give traditional user groups long-term custody rights over mangrove areas. The article says 75% of mangroves benefit from them.
  • Restoration. Restoration means helping a damaged place recover. The report says 3,392 hectares of former mangrove and nearby salt flats could be suitable for recovery. Priority sites are already chosen.
  • Money. The Socio Manglar program pays AUSCEM holders every year for conservation. The report also suggests supporting women-led groups.
“Ecuador has made significant progress in strengthening mangrove conservation and sustainable management.”Elba Fiallo, Coordinator, Coastal Marine Strategy, The Nature Conservancy Ecuador

Shared responsibility is the key idea. The article names communities, national and local governments, businesses such as shrimp farms and port operators, conservation groups and international donors. It says better monitoring and enforcement are needed to close the gap between strong laws and real conditions. It also describes the Mangrove Breakthrough, a global movement. Its goal is to raise USD 4 billion to protect and restore 15 million hectares of mangroves by 2030.

If people take action

  • Deforestation is 86% lower inside protected areas and AUSCEMs than in unprotected areas.
  • 3,392 hectares could recover.
  • Mangroves keep protecting 3.4 million people and preventing about $250 million in flood losses a year.
  • The article says stronger monitoring and enforcement are essential to stop remaining loss.

If nothing is done

The article does not say what will happen. It gives clues:

  • About 8,000 hectares are still under pressure.
  • Laws are strong, but conditions on the ground can fall short.
  • Rangers and AUSCEM holders work under challenging conditions.
