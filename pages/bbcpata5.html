Argentina · Glaciers & Water

The Mountain Ice That Gives Argentina Its Water

A new law changes how glaciers are protected. Farmers, scientists and lawmakers do not agree about what it means.

WhereMendoza, Argentina (Andes mountains)
When11 April 2026
AuthorRachel Flynn
SourceBBC News (Latin America)

MENDOZA, Argentina — Virginia de Valle grows grapes in the shadow of the Andes mountains. “Without water, there would be no wine,” she says. This week, Argentina’s lawmakers passed changes that make the protection of glaciers weaker. De Valle now fears that the water supply for her family’s vineyard is under threat.

Past & Present: Comparing the Ecosystem

This is a mountain ecosystem. The Andes mountains get snow in winter. They also have glaciers. A glacier is a permanent body of dense ice. Snow and ice feed rivers and streams that flow into the valley. Farmers use this water to irrigate their crops. People also use it in their homes.

“Every drop of water counts.”Virginia de Valle, winemaker, Mendoza

Mendoza is a semi-arid province, which means it is fairly dry. Snow is the main water source there. But in years with very little rain and snow, melted glacier water helps during a drought. A drought is a time with too little water. Droughts are becoming more common in Mendoza.

Glaciers matter far beyond Mendoza. Here are some facts:

  • Argentina has 16,968 glaciers.
  • They give water to 36 river basins in 12 provinces.
  • Seven million people live in those provinces.

Then: In 2010, Argentina became the first country in the world with a law made to protect glaciers. The law called glaciers vital water reserves. It banned commercial activity that could damage them. It also protected the periglacial environment. This includes permafrost, which is water trapped in frozen soil.

Now: In April 2026, lawmakers passed reforms that loosen this protection. Glaciers are listed on a national inventory, which is an official list. Ianigla, a snow and ice research institute, keeps it. Now each province can decide whether its glaciers are “strategic.”

Who lives in the Andes near Mendoza? High in the Andes near Mendoza, the guanaco lives in wild areas. A guanaco is a wild relative of the llama. Pumas, Andean foxes and Andean condors live there too. The plants there have adapted to the harsh mountain weather. In winter, snow covers them for many months.

Human Impact: What Changed and Why?

A pressure is something that pushes people to make a choice. Here are the pressures:

  • Mining money. President Javier Milei sees the Andes mountains as a way to bring in billions of dollars from mining companies. Companies such as Glencore, Lundin and BHP Group have visited him. Together with others, they want to invest about $40bn in Argentina’s copper industry, a Bloomberg report says.
  • Provincial governments. Some say the old law stopped them from “promoting a sustainable economic development.”
  • Worry about water. People against the change say large-scale mining could change how rivers flow from the Andes. They say this could threaten water security for millions. Water security means having enough water.

There are two sides to the argument.

Supporters of the change say…

  • The 2010 law was an unnecessary barrier to mining projects.
  • Copper and lithium projects will boost local economies and the country’s energy transition.
  • Milei’s party says glaciers proven to matter for water will still be protected.
  • Federico Palavecino, a lawyer who advises mining projects, asks: “Why should we tell them how to live?”

People against the change say…

  • Large-scale mining could threaten water for millions.
  • Agostina Rossi Serra, a Greenpeace biologist, fears a “race to deregulation.” Companies may pick the province with the fewest rules.
  • The reform is based on “false arguments,” says Lucas Ruiz, a glaciologist.
“If it’s a glacier, it has ice and contributes water.”Lucas Ruiz, glaciologist and independent researcher at Ianigla

Ruiz says the new rules are unclear. He says it is not known what criteria will be used. In his words, any glacier could be at risk.

Climate Change & Consequences

What makes glaciers melt? Heat melts ice. When the air gets warmer, glaciers melt faster. Warmer air also means less of the winter snow that normally adds new ice to a glacier. Scientists say Earth is warming because people burn fossil fuels like coal and oil. This adds extra heat-trapping gases to the air. That is called climate change, and scientists link it to melting glaciers around the world.

What is happening now:

  • Droughts are increasingly common in Mendoza.
  • Glacier water helps vineyards and homes when rain and snow are low.
  • Seven million people live in provinces that get water from glaciers.

What scientists warn about: Ruiz says glaciers are melting fast. It is “highly likely,” he says, that Europe will be almost glacier-free by the end of the century. He says the same for the tropical Andes in Peru and large areas of the Southern Andes. He says the only way to prevent this is to reduce our carbon footprint.

Ruiz also describes a “stark paradox.” He says the world needs an energy transition. He says this cannot happen without more copper and lithium. Mining for these metals could put glaciers at risk. He adds that any mining must be responsible. Its impact on glaciers must be carefully checked first.

“It will affect wineries, but first, it will affect life.”Virginia de Valle, winemaker, Mendoza

Solutions & Shared Responsibility

Many people have a part to play:

  • The law. The 2010 law protected glaciers. Now provincial governments must decide which glaciers are strategic. A glacier is strategic if it gives water for people or farms, helps biodiversity, gives scientific information or attracts tourists. If a province decides a glacier is not strategic, it can be removed from the list. Then it loses its protection.
  • Citizens. More than 100,000 people signed up for a public hearing in March. Fewer than 400 could speak. Protests took place across Argentina. The slogan “Los glaciares no se tocan” means “hands off the glaciers.”
  • Scientists. Ruiz says mining must be responsible and its impact on glaciers carefully assessed.
  • Local people. De Valle talks to visitors at her vineyard about the law change.

If people take action

  • Public voices were heard: 100,000+ people signed up for the hearing.
  • Ruiz says any mining must be responsible, with the impact on glaciers thoroughly assessed.
  • Provinces will make the choices, and Palavecino says they will face the results if things go wrong.

If nothing is done

Exactly what will happen is not known. There are these clues:

  • Ruiz says any glacier could be at risk.
  • People against the law say mining could change river flow and threaten water for millions.
  • Ruiz says glaciers are melting and many could disappear by the end of the century.

Journal prediction: What do you think will happen to Mendoza’s water in ten years? Write your own prediction in your journal. Use two clues.

What Happened Next?

Argentina’s Senate approved the change in February 2026. On 9 April 2026, the lower house voted 137 to 111 to approve it, so it became law. Environmental groups said they would challenge the law in court, and protests continued. We did not find reports yet on which glaciers the provinces have decided to protect or remove from the list.
