Abrolhos Suffered Significant Coral Loss in Less than Two Decades

  • Fire coral nearly disappeared, while brain coral population dropped by half
  • Ocean heatwaves combined with pollution and local deforestation are harming reef health

Jéssica Maes
São Paulo

Coral reefs in Abrolhos, in southern Bahia, are experiencing severe impacts from climate change, pollution and deforestation. An 18-year study shows that species essential to the ecological balance of the South Atlantic have suffered drastic population declines — in some cases nearly disappearing, such as fire coral.

Published on the 18th in the journal Proceedings of the Royal Society, the study analyzed monitoring data collected between 2006 and 2023, a period marked by three mass bleaching events, two global and one local.

The Abrolhos Bank covers 46,000 square kilometers along Brazil’s coast and hosts the largest and most diverse coral reefs in the South Atlantic. Historically less affected by thermal stress, the region has experienced increasing marine heatwaves since 2010.

Researchers found that fire coral, a species found only in Brazil, is now in a state of collapse in the region. Its coverage was already low (less than 2%) at the start of the study and declined further after 2017, the peak of a marine heatwave that began in 2016 and triggered the third global bleaching event.

These branching corals, shaped like small trees, serve as nurseries for fish and invertebrates.

High temperatures in 2017 also caused a sharp decline in Bahia brain coral, an endemic species whose population fell by 45%.
