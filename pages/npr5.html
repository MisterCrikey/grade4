A Desert Wakes Up: Chile’s Atacama Bursts Into Bloom

Heavy rain has brought the biggest flower show in 30 years to one of the driest places on Earth.

WhereCopiapó, Chile (Atacama Desert, northern Chile)
WhenNovember 8, 2026
AuthorJohn Bartlett
SourceNPR (Weekend Edition Sunday)

COPIAPÓ, Chile — A huge part of Chile’s Atacama Desert is now a patchwork of pink, yellow, lilac and purple flowers. The colors stretch across the rocky desert floor as far as the eye can see.

Past & Present: Comparing the Ecosystem

The Atacama is the driest nonpolar desert on Earth. Some areas get almost no rain for years, or even decades. This winter, that changed. Heavy rain soaked deeper into the dry ground than it has in decades. Millions of seeds, bulbs and tubers sprouted above the surface. Many had been dormant under the sand for decades.

Today, more than 200 kinds of plants are in flower. A month ago, the first plants made a pale-yellow haze. Then came a soft purple color as other bulbs sprouted. Now almost every dent and crack in the desert is streaked with color.

This bloom happens every few years, but this one is special. It is the largest and most colorful in 30 years — and maybe ever. Scientists found patches of desert in bloom where flowers had never been recorded before. This suggests that bulbs there have been dormant since before 1997, when scientists first studied the bloom in detail.

The bloom also creates a short-lived ecosystem. Insects hatch. They attract lizards and bats, and larger animals such as the guanaco. One star flower is the crimson garra de león. It is endemic: it grows along just a few miles of this coast, and nowhere else in the world.

“This is a natural seed bank, a dormant archive that becomes a living one with the rains.”Moisés Grimberg, administrator of Llanos de Challe National Park

The blooms last only a few weeks. Then the flowers dry out and release their seeds. The seeds stay in the soil until the next rainfall brings the desert back to life.

Human Impact: What Changed and Why?

Visitors are one big change. Llanos de Challe National Park is a 113,000-acre park on the dry coast. It normally gets about 10,000 visitors a year. During the bloom, 15,000 tourists have visited in just a few weeks. Roads across the desert are lined with tourists’ cars. Chile’s national tourism board expects around 300,000 overnight trips during the two months of bloom. Park staff must protect fragile plants and animals while managing all these visitors.

The bloom also matters for jobs and money. Miguel Vargas, the governor of the Atacama region, says the region’s economy is “heavily reliant on mining.” He sees the bloom as a chance to “diversify” it.

Scientists say the bloom is happening more often because of “changes in the climate.”

What causes these changes? People burn fossil fuels like coal, oil and gasoline. This puts extra carbon dioxide into the air. Gases like this trap heat, so Earth warms. Scientists say human activities are driving the warming seen since the middle of the 20th century.

Climate Change & Consequences

This winter, heavy rain from the El Niño super cycle caused flash floods in the Atacama Desert. During an El Niño, the high-pressure system that usually keeps this region dry gets weaker. This year, storms fed by a long stream of moist air brought one of the heaviest July rains in decades. Some areas got the heaviest rain since records began in the 1950s. Floods in July and August destroyed or damaged dozens of homes. Hundreds of people had to leave their homes.

The same rain brought the flowers. So the weather had two sides: danger for people and new life for the desert. Scientists say the bloom is becoming more frequent as changes in the climate bring unseasonal rains — rain that comes at an unusual time.

“Sometimes people think it’s an empty, arid place, devoid of life, but actually our desert is just waiting for the right conditions.”Ana Sandoval, researcher, Chile’s Agricultural Research Institute (INIA)

Solutions & Shared Responsibility

People are already acting. In June 2023, Chile created a national park to protect a 141,000-acre area where flowers appear every few years. Researchers from INIA are collecting seeds for Chile’s national seed bank. They want to keep the plants’ genetic material and their special ways of surviving in extreme dryness. They also count the flowers in each square meter. This helps them learn how different plants respond to rain.

Local leaders want to go further. Governor Vargas has started the process of asking for UNESCO World Heritage recognition for the bloom. He hopes it will become Chile’s eighth World Heritage site within a couple of years. He says this “would put the Atacama region on the world stage.”

If people take action

  • A national park protects a 141,000-acre area.
  • Seeds are saved in a seed bank.
  • Scientists keep learning how plants respond to rain.
  • UNESCO recognition could help diversify the economy.

If nothing is done

This is not described. The clues: the seeds wait in the soil for the next rain, and the region’s economy now depends heavily on mining. What do you predict? Write your idea in your journal and call it a prediction.

This year, the bloom is expected to last until mid-November. As the flowers fade, scientists have two big questions. Was this the largest flowering event on record? And could a close look find a plant that nobody has named yet?

An update: NASA’s Earth Observatory says this may be the most widespread desert bloom of the satellite era, which means since satellites began watching. Scientists are still studying the flowers. We have not found a report of a new plant species yet.
