Argentina’s Perito Moreno Glacier Is Shrinking Fast, Study Says

Scientists long thought this glacier was stable. New research says it is now in its biggest retreat in a century.

WherePerito Moreno Glacier, Los Glaciares National Park, Argentina
When7 August 2025
AuthorMelina Walling
SourceAP News (The Associated Press), Climate section

PATAGONIA, Argentina — A famous glacier in Argentina is losing ice fast. For a long time, scientists thought it was one of the few glaciers on Earth that stayed stable. New research says the Perito Moreno Glacier is now in its “most substantial retreat in the past century.”

Past & Present: Comparing the Ecosystem

A glacier is a huge, slow-moving mass of ice. The Perito Moreno Glacier is in the Southern Patagonian Ice Field in Argentina. It is in Los Glaciares National Park, near El Calafate. It ends at Lake Argentino. UNESCO named it a World Heritage Site in 1981. Hundreds of thousands of people visit every year.

Here is how the glacier has changed:

  • Past: For decades, the glacier was wedged securely in a valley. For a long time, it was not retreating when most other glaciers were.
  • Now: It has started losing contact with the bedrock, the solid rock under the ice. This makes it shed more ice. It moves backward in a retreat. A retreat means the glacier gets shorter.
  • Soon: The researchers expect it to retreat several more kilometers in the next few years.

The glacier is also known to calve. This means chunks of ice break off and fall into Lake Argentino. Timelapse photos since 2020 show the change.

Climate Change & Consequences

The scientists say the glacier is reacting to climate change. Koch says the glacier has not been climatically stable for over a decade. He calls the retreat a “very delayed response to climate change.” That means the change took a long time to show up.

Richard Alley is an ice scientist at Pennsylvania State University. He says the basic idea is simple: heat melts ice, and global warming means more and faster melting. But he says it is hard to predict when and how glaciers break apart.

Erin Pettit, a glaciologist at Oregon State University, adds one idea. Glaciers change a little even without climate change. But if the climate were stable, snow and ice would offset the melting and movement.

The article names these consequences of glacier melting:

  • Higher oceans. Melting could cause catastrophic sea level rise. This means ocean water gets higher. It could harm and displace people in coastal and island areas.
  • Water and danger. Glaciers can be a source of drinking water. When they collapse, they can leave mudslides.
  • Culture and landscape. Ice carved many landscapes that people love. Glaciers are tied to many cultures.

Alley says the bigger worry is Antarctica. Studies like this one help scientists understand “what might happen to the big guys.”

The article does not give solutions, laws or projects to stop the melting. It does show one thing people are doing: careful research. Koch’s team gathered data in the field. Alley says studies like this help us learn about much bigger ice masses.
