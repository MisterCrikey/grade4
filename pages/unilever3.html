Ecuador’s Mangroves Get a Five-Year Rescue Plan

Conservation International Ecuador and Unilever want to protect and restore mangrove forests in the Gulf of Guayaquil.

WhereGulf of Guayaquil, Ecuador
WhenAugust 26, 2026
AuthorChloe Williment
SourceSustainability Magazine (sustainabilitymag.com)

ECUADOR — Along the coast of Ecuador, a five-year project called Living Gulf aims to protect and restore mangrove forests. It is a nature-based solution: it uses nature to help people and the climate.

Past & Present: Comparing the Ecosystem

Mangroves are plants that can live in very salty water. The article says they can survive in water up to 100 times saltier than most plants. In Ecuador, mangroves cover more than half of the coastline. They support fisheries and the livelihoods of hundreds of thousands of families.

Most of these forests are in one place. The Gulf of Guayaquil has about 80% of Ecuador’s mangroves. They hold a carbon reserve of more than 440 million tonnes.

Mangroves also protect people. The article says dense mangrove forests absorb wave energy and stop shorelines from wearing away. This protects millions of people from floods and storms.

Then and now: Almost 25% of Ecuador’s mangroves have been lost over the past 50 years. So there is less mangrove forest today than 50 years ago.

Who lives in the mangroves? Ecuador’s mangrove trees include the red, black and white mangrove. Red mangrove crabs live in muddy burrows among the roots. Shrimp, oysters, conchs, mussels and fish such as mullet grow up there. More than 40 kinds of birds, including brown pelicans and frigatebirds, live in the forests too.

Human Impact: What Changed and Why?

The article names one main cause of the loss. Almost 25% of the mangroves disappeared over 50 years, largely because shrimp farming expanded.

This matters for people. Many families earn money from fishing, and the fisheries depend on mangroves. When mangroves are lost, these families’ livelihoods are at stake.

How do shrimp farms take the place of mangroves? Shrimp are raised in big ponds dug along the coast. This is the flat, muddy land where mangroves grow. To build the ponds, farmers clear the mangrove trees. Nearly a quarter of Ecuador’s mangroves have been cleared in recent decades, much of it for shrimp ponds. Shrimp is a very big business in Ecuador. Now some shrimp farms are working to bring mangroves back. In June 2026, shrimp industry groups agreed to restore 250 hectares of mangrove inside shrimp farms by 2030.

Climate Change & Consequences

The article says mangroves are becoming more important as climate risks grow along coasts. Two reasons stand out:

  • They store carbon. Living Gulf plans to conserve more than 4.8 million tonnes of carbon stored in these ecosystems.
  • They protect coasts. Dense mangrove forests help protect people from floods and storms.

Living Gulf is expected to benefit around 3,000 people in coastal communities. It also aims to help tackle climate change and protect biodiversity.

What is climate change doing to coasts? Rising seas and stronger storms put coasts at risk. They can also make it harder for mangroves to survive. Mangrove roots help by slowing waves and soaking up extra rain, which lowers the chance of floods.

Solutions & Shared Responsibility

Living Gulf: Mangroves for People, Climate and Nature is led by Conservation International Ecuador and Unilever. Its goals are:

  • Conserve at least 30,000 hectares of mangroves and restore another 2,600 hectares.
  • Work in eight municipalities that depend on mangroves, such as Guayaquil, Machala and Naranjal.
  • Set up 15 mangrove nurseries that can grow about 1.2 million seedlings.
  • Train 300 women, support community businesses and create 350 local jobs.

Local people are at the center of the plan. The project supports the groups already managing Ecuador’s mangroves.

“…the communities who have protected mangroves for generations.”Carolina Rosero, Executive Director, Conservation International Ecuador

The work is also growing. Unilever’s involvement helped bring larger public money from the Green Climate Fund. This supports a second project, Mangroves for Climate, led by Conservation International Ecuador with Ecuador’s Ministry of Environment and Energy. Together, the projects are expected to reach more than 130,000 hectares of mangroves across the country.

If people take action

  • At least 30,000 hectares conserved and 2,600 restored.
  • About 1.2 million seedlings grown in nurseries.
  • Around 3,000 people expected to benefit.
  • 350 local jobs created.

If nothing is done

The article does not say. Its clues: almost 25% of the mangroves were lost in 50 years, and many families depend on them. What do you predict? Write it in your journal and call it a prediction.

Who benefits? The article names several winners. Local people can gain jobs, training and income: 350 jobs, training for 300 women and about 3,000 people who are expected to benefit. Nature gains protected and restored mangroves. The climate gains because mangroves store carbon. The article also says Unilever links protecting nature to the strength of its supply chain. A good investigator checks each claim. Who is paying, and who is making the promises?
